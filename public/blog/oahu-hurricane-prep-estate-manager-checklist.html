Hawaii's official hurricane season runs June 1 through November 30. If you own a luxury property on O'ahu and you're based in California, New York, Tokyo, or anywhere else on the planet, that window represents six months of significant vulnerability — and most of it happens without you there to see it.

A professional estate manager changes that calculation. But "we handle hurricane prep" is easy to say. What does it actually mean? What should a competent manager be doing, on what timeline, and how do you verify it's happening?

This article gives you the complete, phase-by-phase checklist — so you know exactly what to expect, what to ask, and what a manager who isn't doing their job looks like.

⚠ Important Context

The average hurricane in the Central Pacific gives O'ahu 48–72 hours of warning before potential landfall. That is not much time. Everything in the "before the storm" section must be completed before June 1, not when a storm is already forming. By the time a Watch is issued, your manager should be executing a plan — not building one.

Why Absentee Owners Face Unique Hurricane Risk

Owner-occupied properties have a built-in advantage in a storm: the owner is there, making decisions in real time. They see the rising water, hear the shutter straining, and can call a contractor at 11pm because they're standing in the house watching the ceiling drip.

Absentee owners in Kahala, Portlock, Lanikai, and Diamond Head don't have that luxury. What they have instead is a time zone gap, an information gap, and a decision-making gap — and all three compound during an emergency.

Stories from the 2018 Lane and 2023 Dora storm seasons illustrate the pattern: owners who didn't know a window had been left unshuttered. Gutters that flooded because no one had cleared them since the last tenant left. Generator fuel that had gone stale. Emergency contractors who couldn't be reached because the estate manager didn't have existing vendor relationships.

None of that happens with a prepared estate manager running a documented protocol. Here's what that protocol looks like.

Before the Season: The May 1st Checklist

Professional estate management for hurricane-season preparedness begins on May 1 — a full month before the season opens. This is when your manager should be running through the following, ideally with a written report to you confirming completion.

Pre-Season Property Checklist (Complete by May 31)

Exterior & Structure
01
Hurricane shutters or impact windows — operational test. Every shutter panel verified to close fully, hardware inspected, any damaged tracks or panels repaired. For properties without shutters: plywood or fabric shutter systems confirmed and stored on-site.
02
Roof inspection. Loose shingles, flashing gaps, and compromised ridge caps repaired. Roof penetrations (vents, skylights) sealed. Documentation photos taken.
03
Gutters, downspouts, and surface drains cleared. Debris removed from all drainage paths. Downspout extensions confirmed to direct water away from foundation. Standing water zones identified and mitigated.
04
Tree and landscaping assessment. Dead limbs removed, overhanging branches above the structure trimmed. Large trees within fall distance of the building flagged — an arborist consult booked if needed. Properties in Manoa and Nuuanu with mature tree canopy require particular attention.
05
Outdoor furniture, fixtures, and equipment inventoried. All items catalogued. Storage plan confirmed: pool furniture, grills, umbrellas, planters, decorative items all have a designated indoor or secured location for storm deployment.
Mechanical Systems
06
Generator serviced and tested under load. Oil changed, fuel filter replaced, fuel load confirmed. If propane: tank level verified and delivery scheduled. A generator that fails on hour two of a blackout is worse than no generator — false confidence is dangerous.
07
Sump pumps tested. For properties in low-lying areas of Ewa Beach, Kailua, or any area with a crawl space or basement, sump pump operation confirmed and backup float switch verified.
08
Water shutoff location confirmed and accessible. Your manager should be able to shut the main water supply within 60 seconds. If the valve requires a tool, that tool should be staged on-site.
09
HVAC exterior units secured. Condensing units on pads confirmed anchored. Any removable units (window A/C) removed and stored prior to a storm event.
Documentation & Insurance
10
Pre-season property photo documentation. Full interior and exterior photo set taken — every room, every system, all outdoor areas. Dated and stored in a cloud-accessible folder. This is your insurance baseline — without it, a claim for pre-existing damage becomes your word against the adjuster's.
11
Insurance policy reviewed and confirmed. Windstorm and flood coverage verified — these are frequently excluded from standard HO-3 policies. Deductible amounts documented (hurricane deductibles in Hawaii are often 2–5% of insured value, not a flat dollar amount). Policy numbers, adjuster contacts, and claim initiation procedure confirmed with the owner.
12
Emergency vendor contacts confirmed. Licensed contractor, emergency restoration service, plumber, electrician, roofer — all pre-vetted and with your manager's number saved. Post-storm calls go to vendors who already know the property, not strangers found on Google after a disaster.

When a Watch or Warning Is Issued

The National Hurricane Center issues a Hurricane Watch approximately 48 hours before possible hurricane conditions. A Hurricane Warning means conditions are expected within 36 hours. These are not the same thing — and the response timeline is different.

Hurricane Watch Issued (48 Hours Out)

Your estate manager should contact you immediately with a status update: what preparations are already in place from the pre-season work, what remains to be done, estimated timeline for completion, and any anticipated costs requiring authorization.

Physical work begins immediately:

  • All shutters closed and locked
  • All outdoor furniture and equipment moved inside or secured
  • Generator confirmed fueled and tested
  • Perishables in the refrigerator disposed of or consolidated (power outage protocol)
  • Emergency supply kit confirmed on-site (flashlights, batteries, first aid, drinking water)
  • Exterior photo documentation updated
  • Neighboring properties notified if appropriate

Hurricane Warning Issued (36 Hours Out)

At Warning stage, physical preparation should be complete. Your manager's role shifts to communication and standby:

  • Final walk confirming all shutters secured, no loose items outdoors
  • Utility company notified of property status (some utilities offer priority restoration for flagged accounts)
  • Owner notification with final status: "Property is secured. Here is what we did."
  • Manager shelters in a safe location — they cannot be on your property during the storm itself

During the Storm: What Your Manager Should Be Doing

Let's be direct: no one should be at your property during a hurricane. Your estate manager should not be there. You should not expect them to be there. A professional who promises to "monitor" your property during an active storm is either lying or taking an unacceptable risk.

What your manager should be doing during the storm:

  • Monitoring NHC updates and O'ahu civil defense communications
  • Maintaining contact with you — check-ins every 4–6 hours with available information
  • Coordinating with the emergency vendor list for rapid post-storm deployment
  • Documenting the storm timeline for insurance purposes

The moment civil defense issues an all-clear — typically 2–6 hours after the storm passes — your manager should be en route to the property for the first damage assessment.

After the Storm: The 72-Hour Protocol

The 72 hours following a storm are the most consequential. Water intrusion that isn't addressed within 24–48 hours leads to mold. Structural compromise that isn't documented promptly becomes a claims dispute. Theft from unsecured properties spikes in the days after a storm when police resources are stretched.

Post-Storm Protocol (First 72 Hours)

Hour 0–4: First Assessment
1
Full exterior and interior walkthrough, photos and video. Every room, every system, every exterior face. Dated and timestamped. Do not clean anything until documentation is complete — this is your insurance evidence.
2
Damage categorization. Class 1 (structural/safety), Class 2 (water intrusion active), Class 3 (systems failure), Class 4 (cosmetic). Class 1 and 2 require immediate action; Classes 3 and 4 can wait for non-emergency contractors.
3
Written report to owner within 4 hours of assessment. Damage summary, categorized list, photos, immediate action items, estimated costs where known, insurance claim initiation recommendation.
Hour 4–24: Emergency Mitigation
4
Any active water intrusion mitigated. Tarps on roof breaches, wet materials removed, industrial dehumidifiers deployed. A remediation company should be on-site within 24 hours of any water event — mold can begin forming in 24–48 hours in Hawaii's climate.
5
Security verified. Broken windows or doors temporarily secured. Property walkthrough for theft or vandalism. If the property is unsecurable (structural damage), temporary security monitoring arranged.
6
Utility status confirmed. Power, water, and gas assessed. If gas lines are compromised, utility notified and property flagged for inspection before re-entry.
Hour 24–72: Insurance & Repair Coordination
7
Insurance claim initiated. Claim filed with your carrier with full photo documentation attached. Adjuster visit scheduled. Your manager should be present for the adjuster walkthrough — not you calling from the mainland trying to describe damage you haven't seen.
8
Contractor bids collected for non-emergency repairs. Two to three bids for each scope of repair. Bid comparison sent to owner. Recommendation with rationale included.
9
Full property condition report delivered to owner. Comprehensive written summary covering all damage, all actions taken, all costs incurred, insurance claim status, and projected timeline and cost for full restoration.

Insurance: What You Need Before June 1

Standard Hawaii homeowner's policies frequently exclude two of the biggest hurricane risks: windstorm damage and flood damage. This is not fine print — it's a common and costly misunderstanding.

Before hurricane season:

  • Call your insurance agent and specifically ask: "Does my policy cover windstorm and flood damage from a hurricane?" Get the answer in writing.
  • If windstorm is excluded: purchase a separate wind/hurricane endorsement or a Citizens Property Insurance policy (Hawaii's insurer of last resort is the HPIA — Hawaii Property Insurance Association).
  • If flood is excluded: purchase an NFIP (National Flood Insurance Program) policy. Note that NFIP policies have a 30-day waiting period — you cannot purchase flood insurance during a storm watch.
  • Understand your hurricane deductible. On O'ahu, hurricane deductibles are frequently 2–5% of the insured dwelling value — not a flat dollar figure. On a $3M property insured at $2M replacement cost, a 3% hurricane deductible means you pay the first $60,000 out of pocket before insurance responds.
  • For properties in coastal areas like Lanikai or Portlock — confirm coverage specifically addresses storm surge and wave action, which some flood policies exclude.

Emergency Authorization: What to Put in Writing Now

The most avoidable failure in absentee owner hurricane response is the decision bottleneck: a manager who needs owner approval before spending $3,000 on emergency tarping, and an owner who's unreachable on a flight, in a meeting, or asleep in Tokyo.

Before June 1, put the following in writing in your estate management agreement:

  • Pre-storm authorization threshold: The dollar amount your manager can spend without prior approval for pre-storm preparation (shutters, boarding, tree work, generator fuel). Typically $2,500–$5,000.
  • Emergency post-storm authorization threshold: Separate, higher amount for emergency mitigation (water remediation, tarping, structural emergency stabilization). Typically $5,000–$15,000 depending on property value.
  • Preferred contractor list: Names the vendors your manager will engage — not just "any licensed contractor." Pre-established relationships mean faster response and known pricing.
  • Communication protocol: How and how often your manager will contact you during a storm event. Text, email, WhatsApp — confirmed working before June 1, not tested for the first time during a Watch.
If your estate manager has to call you to ask permission to spend $800 on generator fuel when a Hurricane Warning is issued, your emergency plan isn't a plan — it's a hope.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should an estate manager do before hurricane season starts in Hawaii?
Before June 1, a professional estate manager should conduct a pre-season walkthrough, verify hurricane shutters and impact windows are operational, service the backup generator, clear all gutters and drains, secure or inventory outdoor furniture and equipment, confirm windstorm and flood insurance coverage, and establish a signed emergency authorization with spending thresholds. Properties in coastal O'ahu neighborhoods like Lanikai, Kailua, and beachfront Hawaii Kai require particular attention to storm surge risk.
What does a professional estate manager do when a hurricane watch is issued for O'ahu?
When the National Hurricane Center issues a Hurricane Watch (approximately 48 hours before possible conditions), your manager should contact you immediately with a status update, then begin physical preparation: closing and locking all shutters, moving outdoor furniture inside, testing the generator, photographing the property, and notifying utility companies. The window between Watch and Warning is when most critical preparation happens — if your manager waits for a Warning, it may be too late to complete all tasks safely.
How much authority should I give my estate manager during a hurricane emergency?
Your estate management agreement should include an emergency authorization clause allowing pre-storm spending of $2,500–$5,000 and post-storm emergency mitigation spending of $5,000–$15,000 without prior approval. The specific amounts depend on your property value and risk profile. Get this in writing before June 1 — not when a storm is already forming.
What O'ahu neighborhoods face the highest hurricane risk?
Storm surge risk is highest in beachfront Lanikai, Kailua, Waikiki, and the North Shore. Flood risk is elevated in low-elevation areas of Ewa Beach and Kaneohe. Wind damage concentrates on exposed hillside properties in Hawaii Kai, Portlock, and Diamond Head. Kahala and Waialae have historically fared better due to setback from the shore and mature landscaping buffers, but no O'ahu neighborhood is immune.
What should I expect from my estate manager in the 72 hours after a hurricane?
Within 4 hours of the all-clear: a full property walkthrough with photos and video, a written damage assessment categorized by urgency, and a report to you with immediate action items. Within 24 hours: any active water intrusion mitigated, emergency contractors on-site if needed, security verified. Within 72 hours: insurance claim initiated, contractor bids collected for non-emergency repairs, and a comprehensive written condition report delivered.
