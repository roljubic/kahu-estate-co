License Verification: Hawaii DCCA

Hawaii has specific licensing requirements for property management activities, and they are not always clearly communicated by people marketing themselves as "property managers."

When a Real Estate License Is Required

Under Hawaii law, any person who negotiates or executes leases, manages rental bookings, collects rent, or otherwise handles real estate transactions on behalf of others for compensation is generally required to hold a Hawaii Real Property Broker (RB-1) or Salesperson (RS-1) license issued by the DCCA.

This applies to:

  • Vacation rental managers who collect and distribute booking revenue
  • Long-term rental managers who execute leases and collect monthly rent
  • Anyone signing listing agreements or rental contracts on an owner's behalf

When a License May Not Be Required

Estate managers and caretakers focused primarily on physical property oversight — coordinating maintenance, managing vendors, conducting inspections, ensuring property condition — may operate without a real estate license when they are not handling rental proceeds or executing tenancy agreements. This is the legal foundation of Kahu Estate Co.'s estate management model.

That said: if you're uncertain, ask the manager directly what license they hold and what activities they are and are not licensed to perform. A legitimate operator will answer this without hesitation.

How to Verify

Go to pvl.ehawaii.gov/mypvl — the DCCA's public license verification portal. Search by the manager's name or company name. Confirm:

  • License type (RB-1 Broker vs. RS-1 Salesperson)
  • License status (Active — not Expired or Suspended)
  • Expiration date
  • Any disciplinary history (complaints, sanctions, revocations)

If a license is listed as suspended or has a disciplinary notation — walk away immediately regardless of how strong the referral was.

Insurance Requirements

Insurance is the most commonly skipped verification step. Most owners never ask. The absence of proper coverage can leave you personally exposed for incidents on your property that a properly insured manager would have covered.

What to Require

General Liability — Minimum $1M Per Occurrence

Covers bodily injury and property damage claims arising from management activities. If a vendor your manager hired causes damage to a neighboring property, or a contractor is injured on your estate, GL coverage is what responds first.

Request a Certificate of Insurance (COI) naming you as an additional insured. This is not the same as a certificate holder — additional insured status means the policy extends protection to you, not just the manager.

Errors & Omissions (E&O) / Professional Liability

Covers claims arising from management decisions — failure to identify a maintenance issue, improper vendor selection, inadequate reporting. E&O is particularly important for estate managers overseeing high-value properties where a missed inspection or deferred repair can result in six-figure damage claims.

Workers' Compensation

If the management company has employees (including regular cleaners, groundskeepers, or maintenance staff), Hawaii law requires workers' compensation coverage. An uninsured employee injured on your property can file a claim against you as the property owner.

Fidelity Bond / Crime Coverage

If the manager handles client funds — security deposits, maintenance reserves, rental proceeds — a fidelity bond protects against employee theft or misappropriation. This is particularly important for vacation rental managers handling significant booking revenue.

What to Do With the COI

Review it before signing any agreement. Check the coverage amounts, effective dates, and named insureds. Forward it to your property insurance broker and ask them to confirm the coverage is appropriate for your situation. This takes 10 minutes and is one of the highest-leverage due diligence steps you can take.

Financial Controls & Fund Handling

If your property manager will be handling money — maintenance reserves, security deposits, rental proceeds — you need to understand exactly how those funds are managed, stored, and reported.

Questions to Ask

  • Are client funds held in a separate trust account? Commingling client funds with operating accounts is a licensing violation in Hawaii and a serious red flag.
  • What is the pre-approved spending limit before they must notify you? The standard for estate management is $500–$1,000 for routine maintenance; anything above requires owner approval. Know this number before you sign.
  • How and when are monthly reports delivered? Legitimate managers send detailed written reports, not just verbal updates. Ask to see a sample report from a current client (redacted for privacy).
  • What accounting software do you use? Professional operators use dedicated property management software (Buildium, AppFolio, or similar) — not spreadsheets or informal tracking.
  • How do you handle vendor payments? Direct pay from client reserve? Advance by manager with monthly reimbursement? Know the flow before money moves.

Red Lines

Do not engage a manager who cannot clearly explain their fund handling procedures. Vague answers about "we handle it" or "I'll send invoices when needed" are not acceptable for a relationship involving significant financial oversight of a multi-million dollar asset.

Reference Checks: How to Do Them Right

Most people ask for references and then do nothing with them. Here's how to actually extract useful information from a reference check.

Who to Ask For

  • Two to three current clients (not former — anyone can produce a glowing reference from a past relationship)
  • At least one client who has had an emergency or difficult situation handled by this manager
  • At least one client with a similar property profile to yours (value range, location, use type)

If a manager cannot provide current client references — or provides only email references rather than phone — treat this as a yellow flag.

What to Ask References

  • "How long have you been with them? Have you ever considered switching?" — tenure and retention signal satisfaction
  • "When something has gone wrong, how did they handle it? Can you give me a specific example?" — this is the most revealing question
  • "Do they proactively identify issues, or do you usually find out after the fact?"
  • "How often do you actually hear from them vs. getting the scheduled reports?"
  • "If you had to name one thing they could do better, what would it be?"
  • "Would you refer a close friend with a similar property to them?" — binary commitment question

Online Research

Beyond direct references, do basic online research:

  • Google the company name + "reviews" and "complaints"
  • Check the Better Business Bureau (bbb.org) for any complaints
  • Search the DCCA disciplinary database for any license actions
  • Check LinkedIn for the principals — are they who they say they are?
  • Look for any Hawaii news coverage — scams and fraud involving property managers do make local news

Vendor Network Verification

A property manager is only as good as their vendors. For an O'ahu estate, the vendor network is the operational backbone — and it takes years to build a reliable one.

What to Ask

  • "Who is your go-to plumber for emergencies? How long have you worked with them?"
  • "If my pool equipment fails at 9 PM on a Friday, what happens?"
  • "What is your protocol for hurricane prep? Which vendors do you use for storm shutters, debris removal, and post-storm assessment?"
  • "Do your vendors carry their own insurance?" — legitimate estate managers verify vendor COIs before dispatching
  • "Do you receive any commissions or markups from vendors?" — this is the conflict-of-interest question; the answer should be transparent and disclosed in writing

The Markup Question

Some property managers add a markup (typically 10–15%) to vendor invoices as part of their compensation model. This is not inherently wrong — but it must be disclosed in your management agreement. If it is not disclosed and you discover it later, it is a serious breach of fiduciary duty.

Ask directly: "Do you mark up vendor invoices, and if so, how is that disclosed in our agreement?" A legitimate operator will answer this clearly.

Inspection Process & Reporting Standards

For an absentee owner, the inspection protocol is effectively your eyes on the property. It needs to be systematic, documented, and consistent — not a manager "stopping by when they're in the neighborhood."

What to Expect from a Professional Manager

  • Inspection frequency: Minimum monthly for occupied properties; bi-weekly for vacant; after every significant weather event
  • Photo documentation: Every inspection should include photos of all major systems and any issues identified — typically 8–20 photos per inspection
  • Written report: Delivered within 24–48 hours of inspection, not weeks later
  • Issue categorization: Urgent (act within 24h) / Routine (schedule within 30 days) / Monitor (note and watch) — not a verbal summary of "everything looks fine"
  • Pre-approved vs. escalation items: Clear protocol for what they handle independently vs. what requires your approval

Portfolio Size & Attention Capacity

One of the most important — and least-asked — questions is: how many properties are you currently managing, and who specifically will be managing mine?

Why This Matters

A manager handling 35 properties is mathematically incapable of delivering the same attention as one handling 12. This is especially true for luxury estate management, where each property is complex, each client has high expectations, and issues require genuine judgment rather than checkbox responses.

Benchmarks

  • Luxury estate management: 8–15 estates per manager is the professional standard for white-glove service
  • Vacation rental management: 15–25 properties per coordinator is reasonable with proper software support
  • Over 25 luxury estates per manager: Service quality is almost certainly diluted — systems are doing the work, not genuine attention

The Delegation Question

Find out who specifically will be managing your property. Will it be the principal you're meeting with, or will you be handed to a junior coordinator after signing? This is a critical distinction that gets glossed over in sales conversations.

Ask: "Who exactly will be my point of contact? Will that person be the one conducting inspections, or will they delegate to staff?"

Management Agreement Review Checklist

Before signing any property management agreement in Hawaii, verify these elements are present and clearly defined:

  • ☐ Scope of services — exactly what is and is not included
  • ☐ Fee structure — monthly management fee, any additional per-service charges, markup policy on vendor invoices
  • ☐ Pre-approved spending limit — the dollar threshold above which you must authorize expenditures
  • ☐ Inspection frequency and reporting schedule
  • ☐ Termination clause — how much notice is required to exit, what happens to pre-paid fees, what the manager's obligations are on exit
  • ☐ Insurance requirements — what coverage the manager is required to maintain
  • ☐ Fund handling — how client funds are held, when they are disbursed, reconciliation frequency
  • ☐ Liability limitations — what the manager is and is not liable for; make sure these are reasonable and not blanket indemnification
  • ☐ Dispute resolution — mediation or arbitration clause, governing law (should be Hawaii)
  • ☐ Renewal terms — auto-renew clauses, rate adjustment provisions

If any of these items are absent or vaguely defined, do not sign until they are clarified. A reputable manager will not object to specificity — it protects them too.

Have a Hawaii real estate attorney review any management agreement before you sign for the first time. The $300–$500 in legal fees is a trivial investment relative to the asset value at stake.

Red Flags That Should Stop a Hire

Any of these should give you serious pause — more than one should end the conversation:

  • 🔴 No verifiable DCCA license when services require one — this is a legal exposure for both parties
  • 🔴 Reluctance to provide a COI or named as additional insured — no legitimate operator hesitates on this
  • 🔴 Unable to provide current client references — no established manager lacks references
  • 🔴 Vague or verbal-only reporting — "I'll keep you updated" is not a reporting standard
  • 🔴 Contract indemnifies them for essentially everything — this is unreasonable and should be renegotiated
  • 🔴 Undisclosed vendor markups discovered after the fact — breach of fiduciary duty
  • 🔴 Cannot name specific emergency vendors for plumbing, electrical, or storm response
  • 🔴 Unusually low fees with no explanation — below-market fees almost always mean below-market oversight
  • 🔴 Defensive or evasive answers to direct questions — a confident, legitimate operator welcomes scrutiny
  • 🔴 No written management agreement — any operator running on handshakes is not operating professionally

Frequently Asked Questions

Do property managers in Hawaii need to be licensed?

Property managers who collect rent, negotiate leases, or manage vacation rental booking revenue on behalf of owners are generally required to hold a Hawaii DCCA real estate license (RB-1 or RS-1). Estate managers focused on physical property care without handling rental proceeds may operate without one. Always verify what activities your manager will be performing against Hawaii license requirements.

How do I verify a Hawaii property manager's license?

Visit pvl.ehawaii.gov/mypvl and search by name or license number. Confirm the license type, active status, expiration date, and any disciplinary history. Do this before signing any management agreement.

What insurance should an O'ahu property manager carry?

At minimum: General Liability ($1M+ per occurrence), Errors & Omissions coverage, Workers' Compensation if they have employees, and a fidelity bond if they handle client funds. Always request a certificate of insurance naming you as additional insured.

What are red flags when vetting an O'ahu property manager?

No verifiable DCCA license, reluctance to provide a COI, no current client references, vague reporting processes, undisclosed vendor markups, inability to name specific emergency vendors, and unusually low fees without explanation are all serious warning signs.

How many properties should an O'ahu property manager handle?

For luxury estate management, 8–15 estates per full-time manager is the professional standard. Managers handling 20+ high-end properties often lack the bandwidth for genuine white-glove attention. Ask directly how many properties they manage and who specifically will oversee yours.
